# Investor Profile Relevance and Suitability Lens

**Status:** High-level report input
**Scope:** Qualitative investor-profile and distribution analysis for the current 29-ISIN population

## Purpose

This note provides a qualitative lens for discussing which investor characteristics might be compatible with the strategy. It is not a suitability determination, investment advice, legal opinion, or conclusion about the Trust's actual investor profile. A final suitability assessment would require client records, mandate documents, advice files, product disclosures, and the applicable law at the relevant time.

## Overall assessment

The 29-ISIN strategy is mixed in product type but high in aggregate complexity and potential downside. It should not be described as a conservative capital-preservation strategy solely because some instruments are notes, have stated maturities, or pay coupons.

The strategy might be relevant to a financially sophisticated, risk-aware investor seeking structured income or tactical market exposure, provided that the investor has sufficient loss capacity, accepts limited liquidity and issuer risk, and uses the products as a controlled allocation within a diversified portfolio.

The strategy would raise serious suitability concerns for an investor with no financial knowledge. Such an investor could reasonably mistake a coupon, maturity date, or debt label for guaranteed income or capital protection, and may not understand conditional payments, principal-loss scenarios, callability, issuer default, valuation uncertainty, or exit costs.

## Indicative investor profiles

| Investor profile | Potentially relevant product characteristics | Minimum understanding and capacity expected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conservative / preservation-oriented | Selected fixed-rate, secured, or conventional debt products, subject to issuer, guarantor, maturity, liquidity, and recovery risk | Must understand that debt status and coupons do not eliminate default, inflation, liquidity, or loss risk; complex callable, barrier, and conditional-coupon products should not be treated as ordinary preservation assets |
| Moderate / income and balanced risk | Selected callable, range-accrual, CMS, and structured debt products where the investor seeks enhanced income and accepts conditional outcomes | Must understand payoff triggers, early redemption, reinvestment risk, rate scenarios, issuer exposure, valuation uncertainty, and the possibility that the stated coupon does not compensate for the full downside |
| Aggressive / growth or tactical | Equity-linked, barrier, phoenix, express, kick-in, dual-index, and other path-dependent products | Requires advanced understanding of non-linear payoffs, barriers, principal-loss scenarios, volatility, correlation, concentration, liquidity, and exit risk, together with sufficient capacity to absorb substantial losses |

## Ideal investor profile

An investor for whom the strategy might be relevant would generally have:

- Advanced knowledge of structured products, fixed income, derivatives, and issuer credit risk.
- Product-specific understanding of callable, range-accrual, CMS, barrier, phoenix, express, kick-in, and dual-index mechanics where applicable.
- The ability to evaluate conditional coupons, early redemption, principal-loss scenarios, liquidity constraints, valuation uncertainty, and worst-case outcomes.
- Sufficient financial capacity to absorb partial or substantial losses without impairing core financial needs.
- A defined investment horizon and no immediate need for access to the invested capital.
- Tolerance for issuer concentration, limited secondary-market liquidity, uncertain exit pricing, and reinvestment risk after a call.
- A diversified portfolio in which structured products are a controlled allocation rather than the primary preservation or income strategy.
- Access to product-specific advice that records costs, conflicts, risks, assumptions, and the basis for the recommendation.

Knowledge, financial capacity, and risk tolerance are separate tests. A wealthy investor may lack the knowledge to understand a complex note. An experienced investor may understand the risks but lack the capacity to absorb the resulting loss.

## Two-dimensional suitability test

Suitability should be presented as requiring at least two independent tests:

- **Financial capacity:** Can the investor absorb a substantial or total loss without impairing essential financial needs? Does the investor have sufficient liquidity, diversification, and loss capacity?
- **Product knowledge:** Can the investor understand the product's payoff, risks, costs, liquidity, issuer exposure, and worst-case outcome? Can the investor understand the specific ISIN rather than only financial products generally?

|  | Low loss capacity | High loss capacity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low product knowledge** | Clearly unsuitable: the investor may neither understand nor absorb the risk. | Still unsuitable for complex products: wealth does not replace product knowledge. |
| **High product knowledge** | Potentially unsuitable: the investor understands the risk but may not be able to afford the loss. | Potentially suitable: both capacity and knowledge may be present, subject to product-level assessment, diversification, liquidity, and documented suitability. |

Both dimensions should be satisfied. High wealth does not prove financial knowledge, and product knowledge does not prove the ability to absorb losses.

## Contrast: investor with no financial knowledge

| Area | Risk for an inexperienced investor |
| --- | --- |
| Product understanding | May confuse a note, coupon, or maturity date with a guaranteed deposit or bond return |
| Payoff mechanics | May not understand barriers, range conditions, call features, triggers, or index-linked outcomes |
| Downside risk | May not appreciate that principal can be lost despite receiving coupons |
| Issuer risk | May overlook that repayment depends on the issuer or guarantor remaining solvent |
| Liquidity | May assume the product can be sold at or near its purchase price whenever needed |
| Callability | May not understand that the issuer can redeem the product when it is advantageous to the issuer, leaving reinvestment risk |
| Costs and conflicts | May not identify embedded margins, distribution compensation, service fees, exit costs, or potential conflicts |
| Portfolio concentration | May not recognise shared issuer, currency, underlying, or economic exposures across different ISINs |
| Advice dependence | May rely on the headline yield or intermediary description without understanding the underlying risks |

For an investor with zero financial knowledge, the strategy would generally require substantial education, clear product-by-product explanations, downside and stress scenarios, and documented evidence that the investor can understand and bear the risks. A recommendation based mainly on coupon level, historical performance, or the existence of an adviser relationship would not demonstrate suitability.

## If both suitability tests fail

If the investor is inexperienced, has a preservation objective, and lacks the capacity to absorb losses, deployment of the full strategy would present a strong suitability and governance concern:

- The recommendation would appear inconsistent with the investor's stated objectives and capability.
- The manager should generally not deploy the full portfolio without resolving the knowledge and loss-capacity concerns.
- A risk disclosure or signed disclaimer would not necessarily cure the underlying mismatch.
- Appropriate responses could include declining the recommendation, reducing or redesigning the allocation, recommending simpler products, providing further education, and reassessing the investor.
- Potential consequences may include supervisory review, remediation, replacement of investments, customer claims, regulatory sanctions, or reputational harm, depending on the facts and applicable law.

Deployment alone does not automatically establish a regulatory breach or liability. The outcome would depend on the mandate, recommendation, disclosures, client records, warnings, manager authorisation, product risks, losses, causation, and applicable law. However, failure of both the knowledge and loss-capacity tests would materially weaken the manager's ability to demonstrate that the full strategy was appropriate.

## Distribution and conduct lens

The final report should describe the following as expected industry controls, without presenting them as a completed legal assessment of any historical recommendation:

- Identify the client category and whether any professional or qualified-investor classification is supported by records.
- Assess knowledge and experience for the relevant product, not merely for investments generally.
- Assess financial situation, loss capacity, objectives, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and investment horizon.
- Explain the payoff, issuer and guarantor risk, downside scenarios, liquidity, valuation, costs, and conflicts in a form the client can understand.
- Consider concentration across issuers, underlyings, currencies, maturities, and product families.
- Document the product information, target rationale, recommendation, warnings, client responses, and any decision to proceed despite identified risks.
- Identify and disclose relevant distribution compensation, service fees, and potential conflicts, while keeping retrocession as a separate entitlement and payment question.
- Confirm that the recommending external asset manager was appropriately authorised or supervised for the activity and maintained suitable governance, training, records, and conflict controls.

Swiss FinSA and FinIA, together with applicable FINMA supervisory expectations, provide the relevant regulatory context for client classification, information, appropriateness, suitability, conduct, and external-asset-manager governance. They do not create a universal minimum wealth level or a simple numeric knowledge threshold for every structured product, and they do not amount to FINMA approval of an individual recommendation. The precise legal requirements, including those applicable at the date of each recommendation, should be confirmed in a separate Swiss legal workstream.

### Comparative US framework

FINRA Rule 2111 and SEC Regulation Best Interest provide a useful comparative framework. They require consideration of investor knowledge, experience, financial situation, objectives, risk tolerance, liquidity needs, loss capacity, product risks, costs, and conflicts. FINRA also expects enhanced due diligence, training, supervision, and customer-specific suitability analysis for complex products. This US framework is comparative only and does not replace the Swiss legal analysis.

## Report conclusion to capture

The report should conclude that investor-profile relevance must be assessed ISIN by ISIN and recommendation by recommendation. No profile should be inferred solely from a product name, coupon, stated maturity, debt label, or reported holding. The strategy is potentially relevant to a sophisticated investor with appropriate capacity and diversification, but presents material comprehension and suitability concerns for an investor with no financial knowledge.

At portfolio level, the board message may be stated as follows: the 29-ISIN strategy requires an investor with both high product knowledge and high ability to absorb losses. It is generally inconsistent with a non-experienced investor pursuing capital preservation, while selected individual products may have a different risk profile and must be assessed separately.
