# Tier 1 Cost-Benchmark Research

## Research Status

**Research date:** 11 August 2026  
**Scope:** Tier 1 - Light Analytics only  
**Status:** Eleventh source pass complete; cross-market empirical research confirms that disclosed costs may explain only part of an issue-price gap, reinforcing the requirement to retain an explicit residual rather than assign it to hedging, profit, or operations

This report records the external evidence gathered for the Tier 1 cost-benchmark model described in the [Tier 1 Cost-Benchmark Research Plan](tier-1-cost-benchmark-research-plan.md). It is an indicative research record, not a contractual fee analysis, audit report, or legal opinion.

The research supports a model that separates product costs, investment-service costs, and potential third-party compensation. It does **not** currently support applying a single retrocession percentage to every ISIN or concluding that a payment was made to a particular intermediary.

## Executive Finding

The initial evidence supports the following conclusions:

1. A structured-product cost model must separate product manufacturing/management costs from investment-service costs and associated third-party payments. Combining these categories obscures potential double counting.
2. Potential retrocession or inducement is not an additional portfolio-wide cost layer by default. Where present, it is a third-party payment or sub-allocation within the relevant distribution or service-cost economics and must not be added again to an all-in product-cost estimate.
3. The applicable European framework requires itemised presentation of third-party payments to investment firms in the costs-and-charges disclosure. This supports separate presentation in the Tier 1 model but does not evidence an historical payment on these positions.
4. Academic work identified in the German retail market examines the relationship between issue price, fair value, issuer margin, and hedging cost. It supports segment-level calibration of manufacturing economics, not an assumed retrocession rate across different product types, issuers, jurisdictions, or vintages.
5. The executive memo's current `1-5% of notional` range remains an unverified sensitivity hypothesis. It should not be described as an industry-accepted rate until a source set is found that gives comparable product population, vintage, fee definition, and measurement basis.

The second source pass adds two usable, but non-interchangeable, industry comparators. ESMA's historical EU sample reports an average issuance premium of approximately `4.6%` of notional, increasing to `5.5%` when issuer credit risk is included. ESMA's later PRIIPs-based analysis reports a median SRP reduction in yield of `1.03% p.a.`, of which `1.01% p.a.` is attributed to subscription fees. These figures support separate historical issuance and modern annualised entry-cost comparators; neither figure is a distributor commission or retrocession rate.

The third source pass identifies a public component-level distribution observation but does not support a market-wide range. Crédit Agricole final terms for a 2025 five-year callable zero-coupon note disclose a distributor fee of up to `1.75%` of nominal, equivalent to `0.35% p.a.` assuming the notes remain outstanding to maturity. This is a current, product-specific EU observation, not evidence for the 2003-2007 rate-linked cohort and not evidence of a retrocession share. A Swiss BCGE compensation notice confirms that structured-product compensation is to be obtained from the relevant term sheet rather than from a generic schedule; its published numeric ranges apply to collective investment schemes, not structured products.

The fourth source pass produces two additional Swiss comparators. Deutsche Bank's January 2026 Swiss final terms for a callable range-accrual note disclose a placement fee of up to `3.00%` of issue price, approximately `0.30% p.a.` over the scheduled term, payable to the selling bank as a one-off distribution fee or as an equivalent issue-price discount. Separately, a Swiss Finance Institute study of more than 20,000 SIX-listed products reports April 2012-April 2015 median all-in annual total expense ratios of `0.3%` for tracker certificates, `0.6%` for capital-protection certificates, `1.0%` for bonus certificates, `1.4%` for discount certificates, and `1.7%` for barrier reverse convertibles. The study defines this metric as net margin plus all production and distribution fees. Neither comparator is a historical rate-linked rate or a retrocession allocation.

The fifth source pass reopens the named authoritative repositories prescribed in the plan. A January 2007 SEC-filed Barclays callable LIBOR range-accrual pricing supplement states a `2.75%` agent's commission (`USD 38,500` on `USD 1.4m` principal), with `100%` price to public and `97.25%` issuer proceeds. This is a primary, vintage- and payoff-matched US distribution observation, but the US registration and selling channel materially mismatch the Swiss/EU private-bank cohort; it is a labelled bounding comparator, not a historical portfolio band or retrocession rate. Henderson and Pearson's historical US SPARQS study separately reports an approximately `8%` average issue-price-versus-model-value premium across 69 callable equity-linked issues from 2001-2005. That is an all-in pricing gap for a different payoff family and must not be decomposed or stacked with a commission. The official Swiss Federal Supreme Court decision ATF 138 III 755 confirms that distribution compensation can be percentage-of-assets mechanics and applies its mandate analysis to structured products, but its only `1%` figure is the claimant's assumption; it supplies no structured-product market rate.

The sixth source pass completes AC-02's full text and adds one Swiss historical comparator. Bauer, Fink and Stoller's freely available *Forecasting* article verifies, on a reproducible 714-product sample of German DAX discount and capped bonus certificates issued 30 September-31 October 2015, that issuers' disclosed Issuer Estimated Value gross margins (`0.40% p.a.` mean for discount certificates, `1.10% p.a.` mean for capped bonus certificates) are broadly consistent with independently modelled option-pricing values once hedging costs and credit risk are included. This upgrades AC-02 from an abstract-only placeholder to a verified modern German equity-linked issuer-margin source, but it remains a different product family, jurisdiction, and vintage from the portfolio's dominant rate-linked cohort. Separately, a targeted search for the plan's named Swiss historical literature located Wohlwend, Burth and Kraus's May 2001 study of 275 Swiss primary-market structured products; its own quantitative results were not retrievable from the accessible SSRN abstract, but an independent working paper reports an approximately `2%` average overpricing figure attributed to this study, and another secondary citation places multiple contemporaneous surveys, including this one, in a `2%-7%` range. This is citation-sourced, not confirmed from the primary paper's own tables, and is logged as a capped, context-only Swiss historical comparator pending direct access to the original results.

The seventh source pass adds a primary, payoff-matched decomposition template. JPMorgan's SEC-filed May 2025 pricing supplement for callable range-accrual notes linked to the 10-year Constant Maturity Treasury rate reports a `USD 1,000` public price, a `USD 954` issuer estimated value, and a `USD 20` selling commission paid to UBS Financial Services. The remaining `USD 26` (`2.60%` of principal) is not a manufacturing margin: the filing defines it as the combined projected hedging profit or loss, estimated hedging cost, and any third-party data-analytics or electronic-platform fee. Thus the observed `4.60%` issue-price/estimated-value gap can be recorded as `2.00%` directly evidenced distribution compensation plus a `2.60%` explicitly unresolved embedded-economics residual. This is a single, modern US issue and cannot establish a historical Swiss/EU band, an issuer-manufacturing amount, or an onward retrocession allocation, but it is direct evidence for showing rather than silently assigning the residual in any proxy decomposition.

The eighth source pass tested the plan's focused EDGAR expansion across independent issuers and the portfolio's target vintage. Bank of America (May 2004) discloses a `1.00%` agent commission on five-year callable six-month-LIBOR range-accrual notes; UBS (February 2006) discloses a `1.00%` underwriting discount on two-year callable daily range-accrual notes linked to the 10-year CMT rate; and Citigroup (April 2008) discloses a `USD 15` per `USD 1,000` (`1.50%`) dealer concession and Smith Barney adviser sales commission on callable CMS-spread range-accrual notes. The observations are independently issued, payoff- and vintage-matched US component evidence. However, none provides an issuer estimated value or a formula for the remaining issue-price gap. Citigroup's `0%` issuer underwriting discount and `100%` proceeds also demonstrate that dealer/adviser compensation can be funded inside the distributor chain rather than shown as a deduction from issuer proceeds; the `USD 15` concession and `USD 15` adviser commission describe alternative distribution paths and must not be added together. These sources improve the historical distribution record but do not permit a historical manufacturing/residual allocation or a retrocession inference.

The ninth source pass pursued LuxSE/Euronext Dublin discovery for three early portfolio ISINs (`XS0164480286`, `XS0165220400`, and `XS0169318291`). The available search route returned no exact legacy final-terms landing page, so this remains an archive-indexing null result rather than evidence that the documents do not exist. The Lloyds issuer search did retrieve a UBS-hosted October 2005 term sheet for `XS0231918060`, a ten-year USD callable dual range-accrual Euro-MTN issued by Lloyds TSB Bank plc and distributed through UBS's Swiss private-bank contacts. It states that UBS may sell notes to dealers or other financial institutions at a discount to issue price or rebate part of issue price for their own account. This directly supports the proposed distribution-chain mechanism in a close issuer, payoff, vintage, currency, and Swiss private-bank-channel sibling. However, it discloses neither the discount/rebate amount nor the final terms; it cannot calibrate a distribution rate, identify a payment, prove an onward retrocession, or reconcile the issue price to issuer economics.

The tenth source pass tested the specific question of whether a public Swiss or EU source identifies an onward allocation within a structured-product distribution payment. Banque Cantonale Vaudoise's current client notice is a primary Swiss disclosure of the parent component: for structured products it reports a `0%-1.5%` one-off distribution-remuneration range, a `0.5%` average in 2025, and a `0%-1.0% p.a.` periodic range while reporting no periodic structured-product payment in 2025. The notice also describes issuer payments to a distributor as an issue-price rebate or structuring commission. Separately, the official SFBC 2008 distribution-compensation report describes the financial-product distribution value chain as involving multiple producers and intermediaries, including external asset managers, and calls for product-class calculation parameters and ranges to be disclosed. Neither primary document gives the percentage of a structured-product issuer/distributor payment that is passed onward to an adviser or external asset manager. A potentially relevant independent-manager clause found in a search extract of the BCV notice could not be reproduced from the available primary-document text extraction and is not used as evidence. The result therefore improves parent-component and value-chain evidence only; potential retrocession remains `not separately estimable`.

The eleventh source pass tested whether empirical research can allocate the remaining issue-price residual among hedging, issuer profit, and structuring/operations. The existing German IEV source AC-02 provides an explicit pricing architecture but not an amount-level split: issuer estimated value incorporates finance income and expected hedging costs, while the gross issuer margin combines expected profit with distribution and selling costs. A new Danish study of almost 400 principal-protected notes issued from 1998-2009 separately compares modelled overpricing with seller-disclosed product costs and finds approximately `6%` average overpricing, of which only about half is explained by disclosed costs. The remaining hidden-cost component is significant but not attributed to hedging, issuer profit, distribution, or operations. Its principal-protected Danish products and all-in methodology do not calibrate the rate-linked cohort, but the result independently corroborates the model rule that a residual must remain visible rather than be assigned arithmetically to a preferred bucket. No source retrieved in this pass supplies a numerical split of an issuer's hedging, profit, and structuring/operations components.
